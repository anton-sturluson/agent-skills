# Business Understanding Report Checklist

Read this file before extraction to define the report, then reread it after drafting to verify completion. Each section begins with what the reader should learn, followed by the evidence and analysis that must appear.

Use `[x]` for complete, `[n/a — reason]` for genuinely inapplicable, and `[gap — reason]` when the evidence is unavailable. A checked box means the question was answered, not that the business passed an investment test.

## Writing rules

Explain mechanisms in plain language. Separate reported facts, derived figures, interpretation, and unresolved gaps. Cite load-bearing claims to primary evidence. State formulas and put material adjustment detail in the Appendix. Use `n/d` only after confirming a value is unavailable. Order time series oldest to newest and show units. Substitute sector-appropriate metrics when standard industrial measures mislead. Keep market price and trading multiples out until Valuation. Do not issue a buy/sell/hold call.

Give each idea one home:

- 1.2 owns operating profit and cash conversion; 1.3 returns and reinvestment; 1.5 deployment decisions and per-share effects; 2.3 financing capacity; Valuation price-implied expectations and value.
- 1.4 owns customer-facing competitive advantage; 2.4 internal capability and constraints; 2.2 decision-makers and incentives; 1.5 their allocation outcomes; 2.1 the dependency map; 3.1 failure propagation and the pre-mortem.

Cross-reference rather than restate.

When a Munger tendency materially affects a judgment, add a short inline bias callout at the point of use. Quote the relevant definition from `references/bias-definitions.md`, explain how it could pull the analysis, and state the countercheck.

## Opening

Orient the reader quickly. Explain the business, the period examined, the central long-term tension, and the few financial facts needed to frame the report. The financial profile is a compact view into the metrics spine, not a second version of it.

- [ ] State company, ticker, analysis date, and lookback.
- [ ] Explain the customer need, product or service, payer, money flow, claimed durable advantage, and central risk in plain language.
- [ ] Show only decision-relevant financial endpoints and trends from the metrics spine; cross-reference the full spine rather than reproducing it.
- [ ] Include per-share outcomes and dilution plus no more than three sector-specific metrics when useful.
- [ ] Interpret the profile briefly: growth and mix; profitability, cash, and returns; ownership and resilience.

# 1. Value Drivers

## 1.1 Business model and demand

Explain what customers hire the company to do, who pays, why they choose it, how revenue is generated, and what causes demand to strengthen or weaken.

- [ ] Identify the customer, payer, user, purchase decision, and job to be done.
- [ ] Explain pricing, contracts, revenue recognition, recurrence, and visibility.
- [ ] Separate secular from cyclical demand and need from want.
- [ ] Identify substitutes and the conditions under which customers hire or fire the product.
- [ ] Quantify material mix, concentration, retention, churn, and pricing evidence where disclosed.

## 1.2 Profit and cash anatomy

Show how revenue becomes reported profit and cash, which costs flex, which do not, and why margins and conversion changed.

- [ ] Decompose the largest cost components and explain fixed, variable, and step-fixed behavior where possible.
- [ ] Bridge reported earnings to CFO and FCF or the correct sector equivalent.
- [ ] Identify recurring “one-time” costs, material non-GAAP exclusions, and their effect on economics.
- [ ] Explain working-capital behavior, cash conversion, operating leverage, and the main margin drivers.

## 1.3 Economic returns and reinvestment

Explain what capital the business requires, what returns it earns, and whether growth creates value after accounting for reinvestment.

- [ ] Define the capital base and show the appropriate return measure over time, including material adjustments.
- [ ] Explain returns through margin and capital turnover and reconcile profit, cash generation, and capital required.
- [ ] Connect growth to reinvestment rate × incremental return; compare incremental returns with the relevant cost of capital.
- [ ] Assess reinvestment runway, expected fade, and the relevant industry or business-model base rate.

## 1.4 Competitive position and durability

Identify each claimed advantage, the mechanism that creates it, the evidence it exists, and the forces that could strengthen or erode it.

- [ ] State the mechanism, evidence, replication cost and time, and trajectory for each claimed advantage.
- [ ] Distinguish specific mechanisms—customer captivity, cost, scale, network effects, brand, regulation, process, data—rather than calling everything a moat.
- [ ] Separate the durable customer need from the current product or technical solution.
- [ ] Test disruption, substitution, and mean reversion against relevant history or base rates.
- [ ] Include the strongest contrary evidence in the body.

## 1.5 Capital allocation

Show where incremental capital went and whether management's choices improved per-share value relative to alternatives.

- [ ] Reconcile material internal investment, acquisitions, debt changes, dividends, repurchases, and issuance over the lookback.
- [ ] Evaluate outcomes per share and against the opportunity cost of retaining capital.
- [ ] Assess the economics of only the material uses, including acquisition price and integration or repurchases versus dilution and prices paid.
- [ ] Identify meaningful changes in allocation policy and whether outcomes support the stated rationale.

# 2. Structural Context

## 2.1 Value chain and critical dependencies

Map the value chain and identify dependencies that could impair the business because substitutes are costly or slow.

- [ ] Map critical inputs, suppliers, platforms, channels, customers, complementors, and profit pools.
- [ ] For each material dependency, assess concentration, substitutability, switching time, redundancy, and pass-through ability.
- [ ] Explain what the company must control, what it can safely outsource, and where no timely substitute exists.
- [ ] Explain how bargaining power changed over the lookback.

## 2.2 Management, governance, and incentives

Judge management by decisions and outcomes. Explain who controls the company and what behavior its incentives encourage.

- [ ] Compare prior promises and targets with delivered outcomes, including changed definitions or yardsticks.
- [ ] Explain ownership, compensation, governance, related parties, control rights, and the behavior those arrangements encourage.
- [ ] Distinguish management skill from external conditions and judge the track record by outcomes rather than polish or reputation.

## 2.3 Balance sheet and resilience

Explain financing capacity before stress: resources available, claims on them, and dependence on continued market access.

- [ ] Show liquidity, debt or sector solvency, maturities, coverage, covenant headroom, and refinancing dependence.
- [ ] Include leases, pensions, guarantees, purchase commitments, and other material claims.
- [ ] Explain working-capital funding needs and which apparent buffers would remain available under pressure.
- [ ] Identify the financing conditions or constraints that the risk analysis must test.

## 2.4 Organizational capability and constraints

Describe what the company's resources, processes, incentives, and values make it unusually good at repeating—and what its structure makes difficult.

- [ ] Identify repeatable capabilities and separate them from temporary tailwinds or one executive.
- [ ] Identify opportunities or responses made difficult by processes, incentives, economics, or culture.
- [ ] Explain whether scale strengthens capability or creates rigidity, using where capital, people, and attention actually went.

# 3. Durability, Breakpoints, and Pre-mortem

Identify the two or three most plausible paths to permanent impairment. Trace how each would propagate and quantify the most important severe-but-plausible case.

- [ ] Select two or three company-specific impairment paths rather than listing generic risks.
- [ ] Trace each causal chain through dependencies, revenue, margins, cash, financing, recovery options, and time to impact.
- [ ] Quantify one severe-but-plausible stress case using company history, industry evidence, or an explicit dependency failure.
- [ ] Identify warning evidence that would raise or lower each path's probability.
- [ ] Explain management's realistic response options, their costs, and whose capital or income bears the downside.
- [ ] Identify asymmetric exposures that produce ordinary gains but rare large losses and distinguish volatility from permanent loss.

# Valuation

Introduce price only after the structural analysis. Identify the expectations embedded in the current price and test them against operating evidence.

- [ ] State the market price and date and use only valuation lenses appropriate to the business.
- [ ] Run a reverse DCF or sector-appropriate equivalent and explicitly state the market-implied growth rate.
- [ ] State the implied margins, reinvestment needs, incremental returns, and fade period; test their internal consistency.
- [ ] Compare market-implied assumptions with the structural evidence established in the body.
- [ ] Bridge enterprise value to diluted equity value, including all material claims and non-operating assets.
- [ ] Use ranges and sensitivities and keep the conclusion descriptive rather than issuing a recommendation.

# Appendix

Include an Appendix only when it helps verification without interrupting the body.

- [ ] Include material adjustment bridges and supporting tables needed to reproduce the analysis.
- [ ] Define only company-specific terminology that a reader needs.
- [ ] State material unresolved evidence gaps.
